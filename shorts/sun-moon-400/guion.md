- "400 Moons wide": 1,391,400 / 3,475 ≈ 400.4, literally true — computed from the two NASA fact sheets above
- "About 400 times farther": 149.6 million / 384,400 ≈ 389 on average (range ≈ 363–419 over the orbits) — computed from the NASA fact sheets; the 400/400 coincidence as usually stated: The Planetary Society, "An exquisite cosmic coincidence" (Kate Howells, 2024): https://www.planetary.org/articles/an-exquisite-cosmic-coincidence ; Astronomy magazine Q&A: https://www.astronomy.com/science/why-is-the-moon-exactly-the-same-apparent-size-from-earth-as-the-sun-surely-this-cannot-be-just-coincidence-the-odds-against-such-a-perfect-match-are-enormous
- "Each about half a degree wide" (both ≈ 0.53° on average; the Moon varies more, ≈ 29′–34′, so eclipses are sometimes annular) — NASA fact sheets above; Sky & Telescope: https://skyandtelescope.com/astronomy-resources/astronomy-questions-answers/when-will-the-last-total-solar-eclipse-occur
- When aligned, the Moon covers the Sun; the corona appears; the sky darkens (like twilight ~30–40 min after sunset); a 360° sunset glow on the horizon; bright planets (Venus, Jupiter) and a few bright stars can appear — AAS Solar Eclipse Task Force, eclipse phenomena: https://eclipse.aas.org/eclipse-basics/eclipse-phenomena/ ; NASA Science (360° sunset, bright stars or planets); NASA eclipse bulletin "Sky at Totality": https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEpubs/20010621/text/sky-at-totality.html
- Annular eclipse: ring of sunlight, sky not very dark, corona hidden — AAS eclipse phenomena (above)
- Moon drifting away ≈ 3.8 cm (1.5 inches) per year, measured by laser ranging to reflectors on the Moon — NASA, "Laser Beams Reflected Between Earth and Moon Boost Science" (2020): https://www.nasa.gov/missions/lro/laser-beams-reflected-between-earth-and-moon-boost-science/
- Last total solar eclipse ≈ 600 million years from now; the Moon will be ≈ 22,000–23,500 km farther — NASA Goddard (Richard Vondrak, 2017 statement, via Space.com): https://www.space.com/37627-total-solar-eclipse-earth-moon-alignment-future.html ; NASA Space Place ("more than 600 million years"): https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/total-solar-eclipse/en/ ; Sky & Telescope (≈ 23,000 km, > 600 million years). ⚠ APPROXIMATE: assumes today's recession rate, which has varied over Earth's history; Jean Meeus's more detailed calculation (orbital perturbations) gives a later date. VO says "about"; do not put a more precise figure on screen.
> Deliberately NOT used: "no other planet with moons has this coincidence." Not true as worded: Sky & Telescope counts ~35 other moons that can fully hide the Sun, and Saturn's Epimetheus, Prometheus and Pandora can come close to a near-perfect fit (irregular shapes); Scientific American concludes Earth's uniqueness claim "isn't technically true".

- Sky gradient: a full-frame sky behind the horizon that changes by time of day, as one smooth gradient (no banding; grain on): day (pale blue, lighter near the horizon), sunset (warm orange at the horizon to blue above), eclipse twilight (deep blue overhead with an orange glow all along the horizon: the 360° sunset), and a "future annular" state (only slightly dimmed daylight). - Small human silhouette: a standing figure (~50–70 px tall) on the ridge, head tilted up toward the Sun; no face, no details; optional slow, tiny movement (a head turn) so it reads as alive.
- Corona: the Sun's outer atmosphere around the eclipsed disc: a soft, pearly-white glow ~2–3× the disc's radius, slightly uneven (longer soft streamers on two sides, as at real eclipses), smooth and low contrast, blooming in on cue. No rays, flares or spikes (brand §1, §8).
- Eclipse occlusion: the Moon's silhouette masking the Sun's disc in a ground scene (partial → total → annular), with the Sun's halo shrinking as coverage rises and vanishing at totality, and a thin bright ring when the Moon is smaller. The Moon's silhouette uses the `space` color family (very dark, faintly lit by earthshine), never `#000` (reserved for black holes).
- Stars switching ON in a daytime sky: the Starfield's "switching off one by one" in reverse, for a sky that darkens: Venus first (the brightest point), then Jupiter, then a few bright stars, each a clear ~0.5 s fade-in (no global fade, per the compression learnings). No starfield in the daylight scenes.
- Angle cone: two sight lines meeting at an observer's eye with a faint gold wedge between them, plus a dashed ghost disc that slides along the cone, scaling with it, and lands exactly on a second disc ("same angle"). Could be built on Flow paths.
