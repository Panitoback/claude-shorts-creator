- Seen from far away a quasar looks like a star ("quasi-stellar") — ESA/Hubble 3C 273 (https://esahubble.org/images/potw1346a/)
- Supermassive black holes weigh millions to billions of times the Sun's mass — NASA, "Black Hole Types" (https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/types)
- On screen: a nonspinning 1-billion-Sun black hole has an event horizon radius of ~1.8 billion miles (~2.9 billion km), Uranus's distance from the Sun (event horizon radius ~3 km per solar mass) — Astronomy.com, "How big is a supermassive black hole?" - Black holes give off no light; nothing escapes — NASA, "Black Hole Types" (link above)
- Infalling gas forms an accretion disk; friction heats it and it glows; the light comes from the disk, not the black hole — NASA Hubble, "Quasars" (link above)
- VO and bars: disk thermal emission at 10,000–100,000 K ("tens of thousands of degrees"; "UP TO ~100,000 K") — Sanders et al. 1989, cited in Kulkarni et al., "Science with UVEX", arXiv:2111.15608 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.15608)
- Bars: the Sun's surface (photosphere) ~5,500 °C (10,000 °F), ~5,800 K — NASA Sun facts (https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts); NASA/World Book, "Sun" (5,800 K)
- Meter: accretion radiates ~10% of the infalling mass's rest energy vs 0.7% for hydrogen fusion ("about ten times over" rounds down: ~14× at 10%) — Open University, "Introduction to active galaxies" 4.6 (https://www.open.edu/openlearn/science-maths-technology/introduction-active-galaxies/content-section-4.6); range 5.7–32% depending on spin — Fabian, "Black Holes at Work", arXiv:0906.2119 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.2119)
- Balance: the disk outshines its host galaxy and everything in it (100 to 1,000 times a galaxy of ~100 billion stars; "~100 BILLION STARS" is approximate, galaxies vary) — NASA Hubble, "Quasars" (link above)
- On screen: only about 1 in 10 quasars have strong jets (radio-loud fraction ~10–15%, or under 10% in the 2024 Quaia-VLASS census); jets are plasma launched from just outside the black hole — ngVLA Science Book, arXiv:1810.07564 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.07564); Arsenov et al. 2024, arXiv:2411.19531 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19531); Astronomy.com AGN 101 (link above)
- Almost every quasar is billions of light-years away (bright, nearby 3C 273 is ~2.4 billion light-years away; a few faint ones are closer, so not "all") — ESA/Hubble 3C 273 (link above); Astronomy.com, "Why are all quasars far away?" (https://www.astronomy.com/science/why-are-all-quasars-far-away/)
- Timeline: quasar activity peaked ~10 billion years ago and declined steeply as fuel ran low — Mortlock, Nature 514, 43 (2014) (https://www.nature.com/articles/514043b.pdf); Osmer, "Quasars", arXiv:astro-ph/9901238 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/astro-ph/9901238)
- Timeline: the universe is 13.8 billion years old — NASA SVS / Planck (https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/30133)
- VO and timeline: farthest known quasar (redshift 7.77, July 2026), seen ~670 million years after the Big Bang; its light has traveled more than 13 billion years (13.8 − 0.67 ≈ 13.1 billion years ago, derived) — ESA (https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Euclid/Euclid_discovers_the_most_ancient_quasar_in_the_Universe); University of Michigan LSA (https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/lsa/news-events/all-news/search-news/Discovering-the-Most-Ancient-Quasars-in-the-Universe.html)
