SOURCES:
- A quasar is the brightest, most energetic type of active galactic nucleus, powered by a central supermassive black hole pulling in material — Astronomy.com, "Astronomy 101: Quasars and other AGNs" (https://www.astronomy.com/astronomy-for-beginners/astronomy-101-quasars-and-other-agns/); NASA Hubble, "Quasars" (https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/science-behind-the-discoveries/hubble-quasars)
- "Quasar" comes from "quasi-stellar radio source"; they look star-like in the sky (used visually in the twist) — ESA/Hubble 3C 273 (https://esahubble.org/images/potw1346a/)
- Supermassive black holes weigh millions to billions of times the Sun's mass — NASA, "Black Hole Types" (https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/types); NASA, "Black Holes: Monsters in Space"
- Black holes give off no light; nothing escapes — NASA, "Black Hole Types" (https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/types)
- Infalling gas forms an accretion disk; friction heats it and it glows; the light comes from the disk, not the black hole — NASA Hubble, "Quasars" (link above)
- Disk temperature: thermal disk emission at 10,000–100,000 K (hence "tens of thousands of degrees" and "UP TO ~100,000 K") — Sanders et al. 1989, cited in Kulkarni et al., "Science with UVEX", arXiv:2111.15608 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.15608). Not "hotter than any star": Wolf-Rayet stars reach 100,000–200,000 K (Big Think, E. Siegel, https://bigthink.com/starts-with-a-bang/hottest-star/)
- Accretion radiates ~10% of the infalling mass's rest energy vs 0.7% for hydrogen fusion ("about ten times over" is a rounded-down ratio; ~14× at 10%) — Open University, "Introduction to active galaxies" 4.6 (https://www.open.edu/openlearn/science-maths-technology/introduction-active-galaxies/content-section-4.6). Range 5.7–32% depending on spin — Fabian, "Black Holes at Work", arXiv:0906.2119 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.2119)
- The disk outshines its host galaxy and everything in it (100 to 1,000 times a galaxy's light; also basis for hook variants B and C) — NASA Hubble, "Quasars" (link above)
- Only about 1 in 10 quasars have strong jets (radio-loud fraction ~10–15%, or under 10% in the 2024 Quaia-VLASS census); jets are plasma launched from just outside the black hole — ngVLA Science Book, arXiv:1810.07564 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.07564); Arsenov et al. 2024, arXiv:2411.19531 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19531); Astronomy.com AGN 101 (link above)
- Almost every quasar is billions of light-years away (even bright, nearby 3C 273 is ~2.4 billion light-years away; approximate) — ESA/Hubble 3C 273 (link above); Astronomy.com, "Why are all quasars far away?" (https://www.astronomy.com/science/why-are-all-quasars-far-away/)
- Quasars peaked when the universe was young (redshift ~2–3, ~10 billion years ago) and have declined steeply since as fuel ran low (exact decline factor depends on luminosity; ~100–1,000×) — Osmer, "Quasars", arXiv:astro-ph/9901238 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/astro-ph/9901238)
- Farthest known quasar (EUCL J172902.75+641018.1, redshift 7.77, July 2026): seen ~670 million years after the Big Bang; its light has traveled more than 13 billion years — ESA, "Euclid discovers the most ancient quasar in the Universe" (https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Euclid/Euclid_discovers_the_most_ancient_quasar_in_the_Universe); University of Michigan LSA (https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/lsa/news-events/all-news/search-news/Discovering-the-Most-Ancient-Quasars-in-the-Universe.html)
- Earth is 4.54 billion years old (so the light left long before Earth existed) — USGS, "Age of the Earth" (https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/geotime/age.html)
