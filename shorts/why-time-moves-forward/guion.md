SOURCES:
- Hook "You can't undo anything" — rhetorical hyperbole: things can be repaired, but no process
  runs backward. The body states the real claim ("Time only runs one way"). Framing as in Sean
  Carroll, "What Keeps Time Moving Forward? Blame It on the Big Bang", Scientific American (2010)
- Time only runs one way; every difference between past and future traces back to entropy being
  lower in the past and growing — Sean Carroll, "The Arrow of Time" (minutephysics); Carroll,
  "The Origin of the Universe and the Arrow of Time" (UC Davis talk, 2013)
- "The laws of physics work just fine in reverse" — SIMPLIFICATION. The fundamental laws are
  time-symmetric to an excellent approximation; the weak interaction has a small, measured
  time-reversal asymmetry (BaBar Collaboration, Phys. Rev. Lett. 109, 211801, 2012). It does not
  explain the everyday arrow of time
- The two-particle collision looks equally natural forward and reversed — follows from
  time-symmetric mechanics (same Carroll sources)
- Entropy = (the logarithm of) the number of microscopic arrangements that look the same from
  outside; "disorder" is only a simplification — Boltzmann, S = k ln W (any statistical mechanics
  text); Carroll, UC Davis talk
- Things spread out because there are vastly more mixed arrangements than sorted ones; no force
  pushes them — Carroll, UC Davis talk ("more ways to be high-entropy than low-entropy")
- 100 particles: the chance that all are in one given half = (1/2)^100 ≈ 1 in 1.27 × 10^30,
  "about one in a million trillion trillion" (10^30); on screen as "1 in 10³⁰" (exception
  approved) — direct calculation; the classic box-of-gas thought experiment in statistical
  mechanics. NOTE: counts left/right positions only, an illustration rather than a full entropy
  calculation
- Balance: 1 sorted left/right pattern vs all 2^100 − 1 others — direct calculation
- Entropy "almost always grows": the second law is statistical, not absolute — Boltzmann;
  Carroll
- The universe started with very low entropy (the "Past Hypothesis"); why is unknown — Carroll,
  "Cosmology and the Past Hypothesis" (preposterousuniverse.com); Carroll, UC Davis talk. It is a
  hypothesis/assumption, not an observation explained by theory
- Today: 13.8 billion years after the Big Bang — Planck Collaboration 2018 results (A&A 641, A6,
  2020), ESA
- Star birth is down about ninety percent from its peak (on screen: PEAK 100% vs TODAY ~10%,
  peak about 10 billion years ago) — Madau & Dickinson 2014, ARA&A 52, 415: star formation peaked
  ~3.5 billion years after the Big Bang and declined exponentially (e-folding time 3.9 billion
  years); today ≈ 1/9 of the peak. APPROXIMATE (from the fit). Supporting: GAMA survey (Driver et
  al. 2015): the nearby universe's energy output is about half of what it was 2 billion years ago
- The last stars (red dwarfs) fade in about a hundred trillion (10^14) years — Adams & Laughlin,
  "A Dying Universe", Rev. Mod. Phys. 69, 337 (1997). Order of magnitude
- Black holes evaporate by Hawking radiation: ~10^67 years for one solar mass, up to ~10^100
  years (a googol) for the largest supermassive ones — Hawking (1974–75); Discover Magazine,
  "Black Holes Can Die, But the Process Is Incredibly Slow". Order of magnitude; a 2025 Radboud
  University claim of faster decay (10^78 years) is contested
- Heat death "most likely": the standard expectation for a universe that keeps expanding with a
  cosmological constant; open if dark energy evolves (DESI DR2, 2025, hints at this) — Adams &
  Laughlin 1997; DESI Collaboration 2025
> Not used in this short (parked in the project's short-ideas.md): the gravity/smoothness paradox,
>   entropy figures (Egan & Lineweaver 2010), Penrose's 10^(10^123), Jeremy England's hypothesis.
